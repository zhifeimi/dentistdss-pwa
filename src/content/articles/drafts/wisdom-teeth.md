Wisdom teeth are the last molars at the very back of the mouth. They usually come through between about 18 and 25 years of age, and some people never get them. Many cause no trouble, but because they often lack room they can get stuck under the gum, become infected or decay. Your dentist can tell from an examination and X-rays whether yours should be watched or removed.

## Why wisdom teeth cause problems

Most adults have four wisdom teeth, one in each corner of the mouth, though some people have fewer or none at all. They are the last teeth to arrive, and by then there is often not enough space for them.

A wisdom tooth that cannot come through properly is called **impacted**. It may stay completely under the gum and bone, come through only partly, or grow in at an angle against the tooth in front.

Even wisdom teeth that come through fully can be hard to reach with a toothbrush, so plaque builds up around them.

## Common problems

- **Infection of the gum (pericoronitis).** Food and bacteria get trapped under the flap of gum over a partly erupted tooth, causing pain, swelling and sometimes a bad taste.
- **Decay.** Wisdom teeth and the molar in front of them are hard to clean and more likely to decay.
- **Damage to neighbouring teeth.** An angled wisdom tooth can push against the tooth in front, making it hard to clean or damaging its root.
- **Cysts.** Rarely, a fluid-filled sac forms around an impacted tooth and can damage the jawbone.

## Signs your wisdom teeth may need attention

- pain or tenderness at the back of the mouth
- swollen, red gum around a back tooth
- a bad taste or bad breath that comes from the back of the mouth
- jaw stiffness or difficulty opening your mouth
- swelling of the face or jaw
- food often getting stuck behind your back teeth

## Do wisdom teeth always need to come out?

No. If your wisdom teeth have come through fully, are healthy, can be cleaned well and fit with your bite, they can stay. Your dentist will usually monitor them with check-ups and X-rays.

Removal is commonly recommended when a wisdom tooth is impacted, keeps getting infected, is decayed, or is damaging the tooth in front. Wisdom teeth are generally easier to remove in younger adults, when the roots are not fully formed and the bone is softer, so your dentist may suggest acting early if problems are likely.

## What removal involves

Wisdom teeth are removed by a dentist or an oral surgeon. Simple removals can be done in the chair with local anaesthetic. More complicated ones, such as deeply impacted teeth, may involve a small cut in the gum, and some people choose sedation or a general anaesthetic. Your dentist will explain the options, the risks and what to expect.

## Recovering after removal

Some pain, swelling and stiffness for a few days is normal. To help healing:

- follow the care instructions your dentist gives you
- bite gently on gauze to control bleeding in the first hours
- use pain relief as recommended by your dentist or pharmacist
- hold a cold pack against your cheek to reduce swelling
- eat soft foods and avoid chewing on that side
- don't smoke, and avoid drinking through a straw or spitting forcefully for the first days, so the blood clot stays in place
- after the first day, rinse gently with warm salty water if your dentist recommends it

**Dry socket** happens when the blood clot in the socket is lost too early. It causes increasing pain a few days after the extraction, sometimes with a bad taste. If this happens, contact your dentist, who can clean and dress the socket.

## When to see a dentist

Book an appointment if you have pain, swelling or a bad taste at the back of your mouth, or if you are not sure whether your wisdom teeth are coming through properly. After an extraction, contact your dentist if pain gets worse after the first few days, bleeding won't stop, or you have a fever. Seek urgent care for swelling that spreads, or difficulty swallowing or breathing.
