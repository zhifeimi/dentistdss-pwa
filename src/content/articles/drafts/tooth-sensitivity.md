Sensitive teeth cause a brief, sharp pain when you have something hot, cold, sweet or acidic, breathe in cold air or brush. It usually happens when the softer layer under the enamel, the dentine, is exposed through worn enamel or receding gums. Sensitive toothpaste and gentle brushing often help, but sensitivity can also be a sign of decay or a cracked tooth, so it is worth getting checked.

## What causes sensitive teeth?

The outer enamel of a tooth and the gum covering its root protect the dentine underneath. Dentine contains tiny channels that lead to the nerve. When dentine is exposed, hot, cold, sweet or acidic things can reach the nerve through those channels and trigger a sharp pain.

Common reasons dentine becomes exposed:

- **Brushing too hard**, or using a hard-bristled toothbrush, which wears enamel and pushes gums back
- **Gum recession**, often from gum disease or heavy brushing, which exposes the root surface
- **Acid erosion**, from frequent soft drinks, sports drinks, juice, wine or citrus, or from reflux or vomiting
- **Grinding or clenching**, which can wear and crack teeth
- **Tooth decay**, or a filling that is broken or leaking
- **A cracked or chipped tooth**
- **Recent dental work**, such as fillings, cleaning or tooth whitening, which can cause short-term sensitivity

## Is it sensitivity or something else?

Typical sensitivity is a quick, sharp twinge that stops soon after the trigger is removed. Pain that lingers, throbs, wakes you at night or comes with swelling is more likely to be decay, a crack or an infection. These need a dentist, and sensitive toothpaste will not fix them.

## What you can do at home

- **Use a toothpaste for sensitive teeth.** Use it twice a day. It can take a few weeks to work. Some people also find it helps to smear a little on the sensitive spot.
- **Brush gently** with a soft toothbrush and small circular movements, not a hard back-and-forth scrub.
- **Limit acidic food and drinks**, and have them with meals rather than sipping through the day. Drink water afterwards.
- **Don't brush straight after something acidic.** Wait at least an hour so softened enamel isn't brushed away.
- **Use fluoride toothpaste** to strengthen enamel.
- **Ask about a mouthguard** if you grind your teeth at night.

## How a dentist can help

Your dentist will first look for a cause such as decay, a cracked tooth, a leaking filling or gum disease, and treat it. For sensitivity itself, options can include:

- applying a fluoride varnish or desensitising agent to the sensitive area
- covering exposed root surfaces with a tooth-coloured bonding material
- treating gum disease, or in some cases a gum graft to cover exposed roots
- a night-time splint for grinding
- root canal treatment, in rare cases where the nerve is badly affected

## When to see a dentist

Book a check-up if sensitivity lasts more than a few weeks, is getting worse, or is limited to one tooth. See a dentist promptly if the pain lingers after hot or cold things, hurts when you bite, throbs, wakes you at night, or comes with swelling or a bad taste, as these can be signs of decay, a crack or infection. Contact your dentist if sensitivity after dental treatment continues or gets worse.
