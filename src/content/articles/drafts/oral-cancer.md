Oral (mouth) cancer can affect the lips, tongue, gums, cheeks, floor or roof of the mouth, and the throat. Early signs include a sore or ulcer that doesn't heal, a lump, or a red or white patch. Many mouth cancers are first spotted during a dental check-up. Australia has no national screening program for mouth cancer, so any change in your mouth that lasts more than a few weeks should be checked by a dentist or doctor.

## Who is at risk?

Anyone can develop mouth cancer, but some things increase the risk:

- **tobacco**, including smoking and chewing tobacco
- **alcohol**, especially combined with smoking
- **betel or areca nut** chewing
- **sun exposure**, for cancer of the lips
- **human papillomavirus (HPV)**, which is linked to some cancers at the back of the mouth and throat
- **persistent sores or red or white patches** in the mouth
- **poor oral health**
- **older age**

## Signs and symptoms

Mouth cancer can look like everyday problems, which is why changes that don't go away matter. See a dentist or doctor if you notice any of these for more than two or three weeks:

- a mouth ulcer or sore that won't heal, including on the lip
- a lump or thickening in the mouth, lip or neck
- a white or red patch on the gums, tongue or lining of the mouth
- bleeding or numbness in the mouth
- loose teeth without an obvious reason, or dentures that no longer fit
- pain when swallowing, or difficulty chewing, swallowing or moving your tongue
- changes in your speech
- a sore or swelling that doesn't heal after a tooth is removed
- unexplained weight loss

Most of these signs are caused by something other than cancer, but only an examination can tell.

## How it is found

During a check-up, your dentist looks at the lips, tongue, gums, cheeks and the floor and roof of your mouth, and feels your neck for lumps. If something looks unusual, they may take photos and review it again in a couple of weeks, or refer you to a specialist. A biopsy, where a small sample of tissue is examined, is the only way to confirm cancer.

## Why early detection matters

Like most cancers, mouth cancer is easier to treat when it is found early. Small cancers are often treated with surgery alone, while larger ones may need surgery combined with radiotherapy or chemotherapy, which can affect eating, speech and appearance. Finding changes early gives you more options.

## Lowering your risk

- **Don't smoke or chew tobacco**, and avoid betel nut. Quitline and your doctor can help you quit.
- **Limit alcohol.**
- **Protect your lips from the sun** with a broad-brimmed hat and an SPF lip balm.
- **Ask your doctor about HPV vaccination**, which is offered free to young people through the school program in Australia.
- **Look after your oral health** and keep up regular dental check-ups.
- **Check your mouth** once a month in a mirror, and get anything new or unusual looked at.

## When to see a dentist or doctor

Book an appointment if you have a sore, lump, or red or white patch in your mouth that has not healed after two to three weeks, or any of the other signs above. Don't wait for it to hurt: early mouth cancer is often painless.
