Gum disease is an infection of the tissues that hold your teeth in place. It usually starts quietly, with gums that bleed when you brush, and it can be reversed in its early stage. Left alone, it can damage the bone around your teeth and eventually loosen them. Brushing twice a day, cleaning between your teeth every day and seeing a dentist regularly are the best ways to prevent it.

## What causes gum disease?

Gum disease starts with plaque, the sticky film of bacteria that builds up on your teeth every day. When plaque sits along the gumline, the bacteria irritate the gums and they become inflamed. Plaque that is not removed hardens into tartar (calculus), which a toothbrush cannot remove and which gives bacteria even more places to hide.

Some things make gum disease more likely or harder to control:

- smoking or vaping
- diabetes, especially when blood sugar is not well controlled
- hormonal changes, such as during pregnancy
- some medicines that dry the mouth or affect the gums
- crooked or crowded teeth that are hard to clean
- a family history of gum disease

## The two stages: gingivitis and periodontitis

**Gingivitis** is the early, mild stage. The gums are red, puffy and bleed easily, but the bone and tissue holding the teeth are not yet damaged. With a professional clean and good daily care at home, gingivitis can be reversed.

**Periodontitis** is the advanced stage. The inflammation spreads below the gumline, and pockets form between the gums and the teeth. Over time the bone that supports the teeth is lost. This damage cannot be undone, but treatment can stop it getting worse. Without treatment, teeth can become loose and may need to be removed.

## Signs to look out for

Gum disease is often painless, so it is easy to miss. Look out for:

- gums that bleed when you brush or floss
- red, swollen or tender gums
- bad breath or a bad taste that doesn't go away
- gums that have pulled back, making your teeth look longer
- teeth that feel loose or have started to move
- pus between your teeth and gums
- a change in the way your teeth fit together when you bite

Bleeding gums are not normal, even if they don't hurt. If they bleed regularly, book a check-up.

## How gum disease is treated

Treatment depends on how far the disease has progressed.

- **Professional cleaning.** For gingivitis, a dentist or hygienist removes plaque and tartar above and just below the gumline and shows you how to clean the areas you are missing.
- **Deep cleaning (scaling and root planing).** For periodontitis, the dentist cleans the root surfaces below the gumline so the gums can heal and tighten around the teeth. This is often done over more than one visit, sometimes with local anaesthetic.
- **Specialist care.** Advanced disease may need a periodontist, a dentist who specialises in gums. Treatment can include gum surgery to clean deep pockets or to repair tissue.
- **Regular maintenance.** After treatment, more frequent cleans help keep the disease under control.

## How to prevent gum disease

Most gum disease can be prevented with daily habits:

- Brush twice a day for two minutes with a soft toothbrush and fluoride toothpaste, angling the bristles towards the gumline.
- Clean between your teeth every day with floss or interdental brushes. A toothbrush cannot reach these spaces.
- Don't smoke or vape. Smoking is one of the strongest risk factors for gum disease and can hide its warning signs, because it reduces bleeding.
- If you have diabetes, keeping your blood sugar in your target range helps your gums too.
- See your dentist regularly, so problems are found while they are still easy to treat. Your dentist will tell you how often you need a check-up.

## Gum disease and your general health

Research has linked gum disease with other health conditions, including diabetes and heart disease. The link with diabetes goes both ways: diabetes makes gum disease more likely, and gum disease can make blood sugar harder to control. Looking after your gums is part of looking after your overall health.

## When to see a dentist

Book an appointment if your gums bleed regularly, look red or swollen, or if you notice bad breath that doesn't go away. See a dentist promptly if a tooth feels loose, your bite has changed, or there is pus or swelling around a tooth. If you have swelling in your face or neck, a fever, or trouble swallowing or breathing, seek urgent medical care.
