A cavity is a hole in a tooth caused by tooth decay. Decay happens when bacteria in plaque feed on sugar and make acid, and the acid slowly dissolves the tooth's hard outer layer. Caught early, decay can be stopped; once a hole forms, it usually needs a filling. Fluoride toothpaste, less frequent sugar and regular check-ups are the best protection.

## How tooth decay happens

Your mouth is home to bacteria that form plaque, a sticky film on your teeth. Every time you eat or drink something with sugar or starch, these bacteria produce acid. The acid draws minerals out of the enamel, the hard surface of the tooth.

Saliva and fluoride help put those minerals back. Problems start when acid attacks happen more often than your teeth can recover, for example with frequent snacks or sipping sugary drinks through the day. The enamel weakens, first as a chalky white spot and then as a hole. If decay keeps going, it reaches the softer dentine underneath and finally the nerve in the centre of the tooth.

## What raises your risk

- frequent sugary snacks and drinks, including soft drinks, juice and sports drinks
- not brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste
- a dry mouth, from some medicines, breathing through your mouth or medical conditions
- deep grooves in the back teeth that trap food
- gum recession, which exposes the softer root surface
- long gaps between dental check-ups

## Signs of a cavity

Early decay usually causes no symptoms, which is why check-ups matter. As it gets deeper you might notice:

- discomfort or a sharp twinge with sweet, hot or cold food and drinks
- a visible hole or pit in a tooth
- brown, black or white staining on a tooth surface
- food often getting stuck in the same place
- toothache, especially pain that lingers or wakes you at night
- pain when you bite down

A painful, swollen gum near a decayed tooth can mean an infection (abscess), which needs prompt treatment.

## How cavities are treated

The right treatment depends on how deep the decay has gone.

- **Early decay (no hole yet).** Your dentist may recommend fluoride treatments, changes to your diet and better cleaning to help the enamel repair itself.
- **Fillings.** Once there is a hole, the dentist removes the decayed part and fills the tooth with a tooth-coloured or other filling material.
- **Crowns.** A large cavity can leave a tooth too weak for a filling, so it may need a crown, a cap that covers the tooth.
- **Root canal treatment.** If decay reaches the nerve, the infected pulp is removed and the inside of the tooth is cleaned and sealed.
- **Extraction.** When a tooth is too damaged to save, it may need to be removed. Your dentist can talk through options for replacing it.

## How to prevent cavities

- Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste. Spit out the toothpaste afterwards, but don't rinse, so the fluoride keeps working.
- Clean between your teeth every day.
- Keep sugary food and drinks to mealtimes rather than grazing through the day. Water is the best drink between meals.
- Drink tap water where it is fluoridated. Most Australians have access to fluoridated water, which helps protect teeth.
- Ask your dentist about fissure sealants, a protective coating for the grooves of back teeth, especially for children.
- Have regular check-ups so early decay is found while it can still be reversed.

## When to see a dentist

Book a check-up if a tooth is sensitive to sweet or cold things, you can see a hole or dark spot, or food keeps catching in one place. See a dentist promptly for toothache that lingers or wakes you, or pain when you bite. If you have facial swelling, a fever, or swelling that affects your breathing or swallowing, seek urgent care.
